One drip every few seconds, right onto the stainless steel, loud enough to notice and annoying enough to ignore for another month. Sound familiar? ### Why that drip matters more than you think

A single dripping tap feels trivial. It isn't, for two reasons.

First, it wastes water, and in older Gloucester terraces with a metered supply that adds up quietly over a year. Second, and this is the one people miss, a constant drip usually means something inside the tap is worn or seized. Left alone, that small worn part can turn into a tap you can't turn off properly, or a slow leak under the sink that rots the base of your kitchen unit from the inside.

Think of it like a small roof slip. Catch it early and it's a ten-minute job. Leave it a winter and you're replacing timber.

**The good news:** most drips are caused by cheap, replaceable parts. The tap itself is often fine.

### Repair or replace? Ask yourself these first

Before you decide, run through a few quick questions. They'll tell you most of what you need to know.

- **How old is the tap?** Under about ten years and good quality, repair is usually the sensible route. Very old or very cheap, and replacing may cost little more than a repair.
- **Where is it dripping from?** A drip from the spout is often a worn washer or ceramic cartridge. Water seeping from around the base or the handle points to worn seals and sometimes a bigger job.
- **Is it a traditional tap or a mixer?** Older taps with separate hot and cold usually have rubber washers that are cheap to swap. Modern single-lever mixers use a ceramic cartridge, which is a slightly different fix but still straightforward.
- **Is the finish shot?** If the chrome is flaking, the lever is wobbly, and it drips, the tap is telling you it's had enough. Replace.
- **Are you planning a new kitchen soon?** No point spending on a tap you'll bin in a year.

If most of your answers point to a solid tap with a worn part, repair. If they point to age, corrosion, or a tired finish, replace.

### What a tap repair actually involves

People imagine a dripping tap is a big ordeal. Usually it isn't. Here's the boring stuff that fixes it.

**For a traditional washer tap:**
1. Turn off the water at the isolation valve under the sink (or the main stopcock if there isn't one).
2. Open the tap to drain the pressure.
3. Dismantle the head and remove the worn washer.
4. Fit the correct new washer and reassemble.

**For a modern mixer tap:**
1. Isolate the water and drain the tap.
2. Lift off the lever and remove the ceramic cartridge.
3. Fit a like-for-like replacement cartridge.
4. Reassemble and test.

It sounds simple on paper, and for a confident DIYer with the right cartridge it can be. The snag is always in the detail. In a lot of older Gloucester homes the isolation valves are seized, the tap is corroded onto the worktop, or the replacement part isn't an exact match. That's when a five-minute job turns into a soaked kitchen cupboard and a trip to three different shops.

If you're handy and the valves move freely, give it a go. If anything feels stuck, stop before you make it worse. A sheared pipe behind a fitted unit is a far bigger bill than the repair you were trying to avoid.

### When replacing is genuinely the smarter spend

Sometimes repair is false economy. I'd lean towards a new tap when:

- The tap is **old and corroded**, and parts are hard to source.
- It's a **cheap builder's-grade tap** where a new one costs barely more than the repair.
- The **finish is failing** and it looks tired next to the rest of the kitchen.
- You've already **repaired it once or twice** and it keeps coming back.
- Water pressure in the house has changed and the tap no longer suits it.

A decent new tap, fitted properly with fresh flexi connectors and isolation valves, often works out better value over five years than nursing a worn-out one. When a plumber swaps the tap, it's the right moment to replace those cheap flexible hoses underneath, which are a common hidden cause of under-sink leaks.

### A quick word on older Gloucester plumbing

If you're in a solid-wall period home or an older village property, be a little more cautious. The pipework behind the scenes is often a patchwork of old and new, sometimes with copper, lead legacy sections, or compression fittings that haven't been touched in decades.

Forcing a seized tap in that situation is how people end up with a proper emergency rather than a tidy fix. These houses reward a careful, experienced hand and the right materials, not a quick wrench and hope. ### Common questions

**Can I just fix a dripping tap myself?**
Often yes, if it's a straightforward washer or cartridge swap and the isolation valves turn freely. The moment anything feels seized or corroded, stop. A stuck fitting on old pipework is where cheap jobs become expensive ones.

**How much does a kitchen tap repair cost in Gloucester?**
It depends on the tap and what's worn, so I won't pluck a figure out of the air. A simple part swap is a small job; a full tap replacement with new connectors costs more. I'll give you a clear, no-hidden-extras price once I've seen it.

**Why does my tap drip again after I fixed it?**
Usually because the wrong part was fitted, or the real cause was the cartridge rather than the washer, or the seat inside the tap is scored. A repeat drip is often the tap telling you it's time for a replacement.

**My tap leaks from the base, not the spout. Is that different?**
Yes. A spout drip is usually an internal washer or cartridge. Water around the base or handle points to worn body seals, and sometimes it's more cost-effective to replace the tap than chase every seal.

**It's not just a drip, water is spraying or I can't turn it off. What now?**
Turn off the water at the stopcock and don't force anything. That's an emergency plumbing situation, and our plumbing team can get to Gloucester jobs like this quickly.
