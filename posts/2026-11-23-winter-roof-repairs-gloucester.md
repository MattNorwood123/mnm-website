## The small faults that turn into big bills

Most roof repairs I get called out for start as something tiny that nobody noticed. A roof does a quiet job up there until it doesn't. The trick is spotting trouble early, before water finds its way into the timbers.

The usual suspects I see on Gloucester roofs:

- **Slipped or cracked tiles and slates.** After windy spells you often get the odd tile shifting out of place, leaving a gap where water sneaks in. One missing slate above a valley can let in a surprising amount.
- **Cracked or missing mortar on ridges and verges.** Old bedding mortar breaks down over the years, especially on older solid-wall terraces where the roof has seen a few decades.
- **Failed flashing around chimneys and junctions.** Lead flashing lifts or splits, and that's a classic spot for a slow leak that shows up as a damp patch nowhere near the actual fault.
- **Blocked or sagging gutters.** Not strictly the roof, but overflowing water running back under the eaves rots fascia and soffit quicker than anything.

**The honest answer** is that a lot of these are cheap to fix when they're small and expensive when they're left. Water doesn't dry out in a roof void. It soaks into battens and rafters, and then you're paying for timber and plaster as well as tiles.

## What needs fixing now, and what can wait

Not every imperfection is an emergency. Here's how I'd rank it when I'm up a ladder having a look.

**Fix it now:**

1. Any active leak showing as a damp patch, a drip, or a bulging ceiling inside.
2. Missing tiles or slates over a bedroom or bathroom, where water has a short route into the house.
3. Lifted or split flashing around the chimney, especially before a run of wet weather.
4. A sagging section of roof, which can point to something structural underneath.

**Keep an eye on, plan ahead:**

1. A single cracked tile that isn't letting water through yet.
2. Weathered mortar that's cosmetic rather than failing.
3. Moss build-up, which is worth clearing but rarely urgent.
4. Ageing but intact felt or underlay, where you're planning for the future rather than reacting.

If you're not sure which category you're in, that's exactly what an inspection is for. I'd always rather tell you honestly that something can wait a season than talk you into work you don't need yet.

## Why timing matters more than people think

There's a reason roofing and emergency plumbing calls spike after winter storms around here. The weather exposes the weak spots. The problem is that winter is also the worst time to do a proper, lasting repair.

Sealants, mortar and roofing adhesives cure far better in warm, dry conditions. Patch a ridge in freezing drizzle and you're often back doing it again. That's why, where the leak isn't urgent, I'll sometimes suggest a temporary make-safe now and a proper fix when conditions are right. ## Older Gloucester roofs need the right materials

If you own one of the older solid-wall or period homes dotted around Gloucester and the villages, the repair isn't just about slapping a tile back on. These roofs were built with specific materials for a reason, and modern shortcuts can cause more harm than good.

On older properties I'll use materials suited to their period, like lime-based mortars where they belong, rather than hard modern mixes that trap moisture and crack the surrounding fabric. Match the wrong material to an old roof and you can accelerate the very damage you're trying to repair. Experienced handling matters here more than anywhere.

## Common questions

### How do I know if I have a leak if there's no obvious drip?

Look for damp patches or staining on upstairs ceilings, a musty smell in the loft, or daylight visible through the roof from inside. Water often travels along a timber before it drips, so the stain rarely sits directly under the fault.

### Can you repair just a few tiles, or do I need a whole new roof?

In most cases a handful of slipped or cracked tiles is a straightforward repair. A full replacement only makes sense when the underlay has failed widely or the roof is near the end of its life. I'll tell you honestly which situation you're in.

### Should I wait until summer to get my roof fixed?

If it's actively leaking, no, get it made safe now. If it's a small, non-urgent fault, the dry warmer months give longer-lasting results because the materials cure properly.

### Do you handle older period roofs differently?

Yes. Older properties often need period-appropriate materials like lime mortar, and a repair that respects how the original roof was built. The wrong modern material can do more damage.
