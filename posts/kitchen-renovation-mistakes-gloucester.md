## Mistake 1: Buying units before checking what's behind the walls

A lot of Gloucester homes are older, solid-wall properties, and what's hiding behind the plaster matters. People fall in love with a kitchen design in a showroom, pay the deposit, then discover the wall they wanted their new run of units on is crumbling, damp, or full of dodgy old wiring.

In a period property that's not unusual at all. Old lath and plaster, tired pipework, sockets that were fine in 1985 but nowhere near where you need them now.

Get the bones checked first:

- The condition of the walls and whether they'll take heavy wall units
- Where the water and waste actually run
- Whether the electrics can handle a modern kitchen's load
- Floor levels, because old floors are rarely flat

Sort the boring stuff before the pretty stuff. It's far cheaper to move a pipe before the tiles go on than after.

## Mistake 2: Getting a vague quote and calling it a plan

Here's where the "save you thousands" part really bites. A cheap quote that leaves out plastering, electrical alterations, waste rerouting or making good afterwards isn't cheap. It's just incomplete. The costs turn up later as "extras," and by then you've no leverage to argue.

A proper kitchen renovation quote should spell out:

1. Strip-out and disposal of the old kitchen
2. Any building work, like removing a wall or forming an opening
3. First-fix plumbing and electrics
4. Plastering and making good
5. Fitting units, worktops and appliances
6. Tiling, flooring and finishing
7. Final clean and snagging

When you can see each stage, you can compare quotes honestly instead of just chasing the lowest headline figure. I'd always rather tell someone the real number upfront than surprise them halfway through.

## Mistake 3: Underestimating how long you'll live without a kitchen

People massively underestimate the disruption. A full kitchen refit isn't a weekend. Between strip-out, building work, drying times for plaster, worktop templating and the fitting itself, you're living out of a microwave in the living room for a good while.

If you plan for that, it's fine. Set up a temporary kitchen corner, keep the kettle and the fridge somewhere accessible, and warn the family. The people who struggle are the ones who assumed it'd all be done in a few days and made no arrangements.

Worktop templating is a classic delay. Solid worktops usually get measured only after the units are fitted, then there's a lead time before they're cut and installed. That gap catches people out every time.

## Mistake 4: Choosing the layout for looks, not for how you actually cook

A kitchen that photographs well isn't always a kitchen that works. I've seen island units so big you can't open the oven, and sinks placed miles from the hob because it looked balanced on paper.

Think about how you move around the room:

- Can two people work in there without colliding?
- Is there worktop either side of the hob and the sink?
- Do the fridge, bin and dishwasher sit where they make sense?
- Are your most-used items within easy reach?

Get the flow right and even a small Gloucester galley kitchen feels twice the size. Get it wrong and a big expensive kitchen feels awkward forever.

## Mistake 5: Spending the money in the wrong places

Budgets are finite, so it matters where the money lands. I see people blow the lot on a fancy branded appliance they'll barely use, then skimp on the stuff that actually gets battered every single day.

Where it's usually worth spending:

- Worktops, because they take constant abuse
- Drawer runners and hinges, the moving parts that fail first on cheap units
- The sink and tap, used dozens of times a day
- Good extraction, especially in an open-plan space

Where you can often ease off:

- Ultra-premium appliances if a mid-range one does the same job
- Trend-led finishes you'll tire of in five years
- Decorative extras that don't earn their keep

Spend on what you touch and use hardest. That's the honest rule of thumb I give everyone.

## Mistake 6: Hiring a different person for every trade

A kitchen touches nearly every trade there is: building, plumbing, electrics, plastering, tiling, joinery. When you hire those separately, you become the project manager whether you wanted to or not. The electrician can't come until the plasterer's done, the plasterer's waiting on first fix, and everyone blames everyone else when the timings slip.

## Mistake 7: Falling for the upsell

This one costs the most and shows up the least on any invoice. Some outfits will find "problems" that conveniently need expensive fixing, or push work you simply don't need yet.

If something can wait, we'll tell you it can wait. If a wall's sound, we're not going to invent a reason to rebuild it. Ask any tradesperson to explain plainly why a job is necessary. If the answer's woolly or leans hard on fear, be careful.

## Common questions

**How much disruption should I really expect?**
More than you think, but manageable if you plan for it. Set up a temporary cooking spot, expect dust despite our best efforts, and know that some days will be quieter than others while things like plaster dry out.

**Do I need planning permission for a kitchen?**
A like-for-like refit usually doesn't. If you're knocking through walls, extending, or altering the structure, that's a different conversation, and we'll guide you through what's needed before we start.

**Can you handle everything, or do I need to book separate trades?**
Everything under one roof. Building, plumbing, electrics, plastering and tiling all handled together, which keeps the timeline tight and the accountability clear.

**Is it cheaper to renovate in a quieter season?**
Workload shifts through the year, with roofing and emergencies busier after winter storms and gardens busier in spring. Kitchens run year-round, so it's more about booking ahead than chasing a season.

## The practical takeaway

Most kitchen renovations that go over budget don't go wrong because of one big disaster. They go wrong through small mistakes stacking up: a vague quote, hidden wall problems, poor timing, money spent in the wrong places, and too many hands with nobody in charge. Fix those and the whole thing gets calmer and cheaper.
