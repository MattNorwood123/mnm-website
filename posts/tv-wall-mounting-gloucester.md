### Start with the wall, not the bracket

Before anything else, you need to know what you're screwing into. Get this wrong and the TV either won't hold or you'll make a mess trying.

Around Gloucester I see three common walls:

- **Solid brick or block.** Common in older terraces and period homes. Strong, but you need the right masonry fixings and a hammer drill to get into it cleanly.
- **Plasterboard on timber studs.** Standard in newer builds and extensions. The board itself holds almost nothing, so the bracket has to catch the timber studs behind it.
- **Dot-and-dab plasterboard.** Board stuck to a brick wall with blobs of adhesive, leaving a gap behind. This one catches people out. Short fixings grab only the board, and the whole lot can pull away.

**Ask yourself these before you drill:**

1. Is the wall solid or hollow when you knock on it?
2. Where are the studs, or where is the solid backing?
3. Is there anything hidden behind it like a cable, a pipe, or a chimney breast flue?

That last point matters more than people think, especially in older Gloucester houses where a chimney breast or a buried cable run can sit exactly where you want your bracket.

### Picking the right bracket and fixings

The bracket that comes in the box is often fine. The fixings taped to it usually aren't, because they're generic and don't know what wall you own.

**Fixed, tilting or full-motion?**

- A **fixed bracket** sits flat and close to the wall. Neat, cheap, and best when your seating lines up with the screen.
- A **tilting bracket** lets you angle the screen down, handy if the TV sits higher than eye level, say above a fireplace.
- A **full-motion arm** swings out and turns. Great for a kitchen or a room where you watch from different spots, but it puts far more leverage on the wall, so your fixings need to be spot on.

Match the bracket to the VESA pattern on the back of your TV (the spacing of the four screw holes) and to the weight. A big screen on an arm pulling away from a dot-and-dab wall is exactly the kind of job I get called to put right.

### The cables are the part everyone forgets

A level TV with four cables dangling down the wall still looks unfinished. Sorting the cables properly is what makes the difference between a DIY look and a tidy one.

You've really got two options:

- **Trunking.** A plastic channel over the plaster that hides the cables. Quick, reversible, and fine if you're renting or don't fancy chasing into the wall.
- **Cables inside the wall.** Cleaner finish, with the leads fed behind the plaster and brought out at a socket near the TV and another by the media unit.

Here's the honest answer on that second option. Running a mains cable inside the wall or moving a socket is electrical work, and it should be done properly by someone qualified, not bodged with an extension lead buried behind plaster. ### Getting it level and at the right height

Two things ruin an otherwise good job: a screen that's slightly off level, and a screen hung too high.

**On height:** most people mount too high, usually because they're standing up when they decide. Sit where you'll actually watch and aim for the middle of the screen to land roughly at eye level. Above a fireplace you'll often need a tilting bracket to bring the picture back down to you.

**On level:** don't trust your eye and don't fully trust the skirting board, because in older Gloucester houses nothing is truly square. Use a proper spirit level on the bracket itself, mark your holes, and double-check before you commit the drill.

### When DIY is fine and when to call someone

I'm not going to pretend you need a tradesman for every telly. Plenty of people mount their own perfectly well. It usually comes down to a few honest questions.

**You're probably fine doing it yourself if:**

- It's a small-to-medium screen on a known solid wall.
- You've got a decent drill, the right fixings, and a level.
- The cables can run to an existing socket nearby.

**It's worth getting help if:**

- It's a large or heavy screen, or you're using a full-motion arm.
- The wall is dot-and-dab, plasterboard, or you're not sure what's behind it.
- You want the cables hidden in the wall or a new socket behind the TV.
- It's going over a fireplace or anywhere a fall would do real damage.

### A quick word on older Gloucester walls

If you're in a period property, the plaster is worth treating with respect. Older solid walls were often finished with lime plaster, which behaves differently from modern gypsum and can blow or crumble around a careless fixing. Proper handling and the right materials make all the difference on these homes, and it's one of those details that separates a clean job from a patch-up later. If your wall feels soft or sounds hollow in odd places, that's a sign to stop and get a proper look before you drill.

### Common questions

**Can you hide the cables completely?**
Yes, by chasing them into the wall and bringing them out at sockets near the screen. If a new power point is needed behind the TV, that part is proper electrical work and should be done by someone qualified rather than run on a hidden extension lead.

**Will mounting a TV damage my wall?**
Done correctly, you're left with four small, filled holes if you ever take it down. The damage I'm usually called to fix comes from the wrong fixings in plasterboard or dot-and-dab walls, where the screen has started to pull away.

**Can you put a TV above the fireplace?**
Often yes, but think about height and heat. A tilting bracket brings the picture down to a comfortable angle, and you want to be sure the wall and any flue behind it can take the fixings.

**Do you do more than just the TV while you're there?**
Usually, yes. Most people line up a few jobs at once, shelves, a mirror, a bit of flat-pack, so it all gets handled in one visit rather than several.

**How long does it take?**
A straightforward mount onto a solid wall with a nearby socket is quick. Hiding cables, moving a socket, or working with a tricky wall adds time, which is why I'd rather see it first than quote blind.
