## Why reactive maintenance quietly drains your profit

Most landlords I meet aren't neglectful. They're just busy, and the property seems fine until it isn't. The trouble is that waiting for things to break is the most expensive way to run a rental.

A slipped tile is a cheap fix on a dry day. Leave it through a Gloucestershire winter and you're looking at a damp ceiling, ruined plaster, maybe a soaked carpet and an unhappy tenant on top. One small job became four.

**The pattern I see again and again:**

- A minor leak ignored until it rots a floor
- A tired fuse board left until something trips and the whole flat loses power
- Guttering never cleared until water runs down the wall and brings damp inside
- A boiler nursed along until it dies in the coldest week of the year

Planned maintenance isn't about spending more. It's about spending at the right time, when the fix is small and you're not paying emergency rates.

## The compliance basics you can't skip

Some maintenance is a choice. Some of it is the law, and getting it wrong is where landlords land in real trouble. These are the non-negotiables to stay on top of.

**Electrical safety.** As a landlord in England you need an Electrical Installation Condition Report, an EICR, for each rented property. The legal minimum is at least every five years, and sooner if the report says the installation needs checking earlier. We're [City & Guilds 2391 qualified](/electrician-gloucester) for exactly this kind of testing and inspection work, so the report you get is done properly, not rubber-stamped. For the full rundown, read my [landlord's guide to EICRs](/blog/landlord-eicr-guide-gloucester/).

**Gas safety.** If there's gas in the property, that's an annual check by a registered engineer. Keep the certificates.

**Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms.** These need to be present, working, and tested at the start of a tenancy. It's a five-minute job that protects your tenant and you.

**General repair duty.** You're responsible for keeping the structure, roof, plumbing, heating and electrics in working order. That's not optional, and a tenant who reports a fault has every right to expect it dealt with.

My advice is simple: get everything on a calendar with renewal dates, so a certificate never lapses without you noticing. If you've inherited a property and aren't sure what's in date, that's the first thing to sort.

## Build a maintenance rhythm around the Gloucester seasons

Rental problems aren't random. They follow the weather, and if you work with the seasons you stay ahead of them.

### Winter and after storms

This is when my phone rings most for roofing and emergency plumbing. Wind lifts and cracks tiles and slates, leaving gaps where water sneaks in. Cold snaps find weak pipework. A quick roof check before winter, and clear gutters going into it, head off most of the drama.

### Spring

A good time for the outside jobs. If your rentals have gardens, patios or paths, this is when tenants start using them and when [landscaping and outdoor work](/landscaping-gloucester) is easiest to get done. Tidy outdoor space also helps a property let faster.

### Summer

The best window for roof inspections and anything involving sealants and mortar, because they cure far better in warm, dry conditions. If you've got a job that's been waiting, summer is when it's done right.

### Autumn

Clear the gutters again, check the heating works before the first cold week, and get ahead of the winter rush rather than joining it.

Spreading the work across the year keeps your costs level and stops everything landing in one panicked, pricey month.

## Older and solid-wall properties need the right touch

Plenty of Gloucester's rental stock is older, solid-wall terraces and period homes in the villages. These properties are lovely, but they don't behave like a modern new-build, and this is where I see other trades get it wrong.

Solid-wall homes need to breathe. Patch them up with the wrong modern materials, cement render or the wrong plaster, and you trap moisture inside the wall. The result is damp that looks like a leak but is actually the repair itself.

These places want proper period-appropriate materials, lime among them, and someone who understands why. If you've got an older rental, use a tradesperson who's handled them before. The right material at the start costs less than ripping out a bad repair later.

## Small jobs are where good landlords win

The difference between a property that holds its tenants and one with constant turnover often comes down to how quickly the small stuff gets sorted. A dripping tap, a sticking door, a loose rail, a blown socket.

None of it is dramatic, but a tenant who sees repairs handled quickly tends to stay, look after the place, and renew.

This is exactly the sort of work I like getting from property managers and maintenance teams: a steady list of small tasks, done properly, so nothing escalates. It keeps tenants happy and keeps your bigger bills away.

**A quick between-tenancy checklist worth running:**

1. Test every smoke and CO alarm
2. Check all taps, toilets and under-sink pipes for drips
3. Run the heating and hot water
4. Look at the roofline and gutters from the ground
5. Confirm the EICR and gas certificate are still in date
6. Fix the little cosmetic bits before the viewing, not after

Fifteen minutes of checking saves a mid-tenancy emergency.
