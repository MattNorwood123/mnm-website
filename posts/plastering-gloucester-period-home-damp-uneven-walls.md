### Why period walls behave differently

Most newer homes are built with cavity walls, two skins with a gap between them. A huge chunk of Gloucester's older housing stock is solid wall, often brick or stone with no cavity at all. That changes everything about how moisture moves.

Solid walls are designed to breathe. They take on a little moisture and then let it dry back out again, usually through the plaster and the pointing. When that breathing gets blocked, the damp has nowhere to go, so it sits in the wall and shows up as blown plaster, salty tide marks, or that musty smell you can never quite place.

That's the lesson other trades miss. **Older, solid-wall properties need materials designed for their period.** On the right walls that means lime-based products rather than modern cement or standard gypsum, because lime lets the wall keep breathing instead of trapping water behind a hard, waterproof skin.

### What plastering can genuinely fix

Plastering is brilliant at the cosmetic and the structural-surface stuff. On a period wall, a proper job will sort out:

- **Uneven, wavy walls** that have moved and settled over a century or more, bringing them back to a flat, paintable finish.
- **Cracked, hollow or blown plaster** where the old layer has lost its grip on the wall behind it.
- **Rough patches** left behind after old chimney breasts, removed fittings, or botched DIY repairs.
- **A breathable finish** on a solid wall when the right lime products are used, so the surface works with the building rather than against it.
- **A clean canvas** before decorating, tiling, or a full kitchen or bathroom refit.

If your wall is sound, dry and just tired-looking, plastering is exactly the right answer. You'll get a flat, smooth, long-lasting finish that makes the whole room feel new.

### What plastering can't fix (the bit people don't want to hear)

Here's the honest answer. **Plaster is a finish, not a cure.** If you skim straight over an active damp problem, the moisture is still there. Within weeks or months it pushes back through, and you're looking at blown plaster, flaking paint and salts all over again, except now you've paid for it twice.

Plastering will not fix:

- A leaking roof or slipped tile letting water into the wall from above.
- A dripping waste pipe or a failed seal behind a bathroom.
- Blocked or overflowing gutters soaking one patch of wall every time it rains.
- Ground levels or hard cement render trapping moisture in the base of the wall.
- Condensation caused by poor ventilation, which is more common than people think in tightly sealed-up older rooms.

Find the cause first, fix it, let the wall dry, then plaster. Do it in that order and the finish lasts. Skip the boring stuff before the pretty stuff and you're just hiding the problem for a season.

### How to work out what's actually going on

Before you book anyone, ask yourself these:

1. **Where is the damp?** High up near the ceiling often points to the roof or gutters. Low down near the skirting is more likely ground-related or trapped moisture.
2. **Does it get worse after rain or after cold nights?** Rain-driven damp suggests water getting in from outside. Cold-night, window-and-corner damp usually means condensation and ventilation.
3. **Is there a tide mark or white, fluffy salts?** That's moisture moving through the wall and evaporating at the surface, a sign something needs tracing, not just skimming.
4. **Has anyone slapped cement render or waterproof paint on a solid wall?** On a breathable wall that can trap moisture and make things worse.

### Lime versus gypsum and cement on an old wall

This is where the right choice saves you real money long term.

**Think of a solid wall like a wax jacket that needs to breathe.** Lime plaster is breathable. It lets small amounts of moisture pass through and dry out naturally, which is how these walls were always meant to work. Modern gypsum and especially cement are much less forgiving. Seal a damp-prone period wall behind them and the moisture gets trapped, then blows the plaster off.

That doesn't mean every wall in every old house needs lime. Plenty of rooms are perfectly dry and stable and take a standard finish just fine. The skill is knowing which wall you're standing in front of, and that comes from experience with Gloucester's older stock rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

### Common questions

**Can you just plaster over damp to sell the house quickly?**
You can, but I wouldn't. It looks fine for a few weeks, then comes back, and a decent surveyor or buyer will spot fresh patchy plaster for what it is. Fixing the cause is the honest route.

**How long should a wall dry before plastering?**
It depends on how wet it got and the time of year, so there's no single answer. The key is that the source is fixed and the wall is genuinely drying out, not still taking on water.

**Is lime plaster more expensive?**
Lime work can take more time and skill, so it can cost more up front. On the right wall it saves money overall because you're not redoing a failed modern skim a year later.
