The usual story goes like this. ## The jobs that keep a rental legal and lettable

Some maintenance is about keeping tenants happy. Some of it is about keeping you on the right side of the law and the insurance. It helps to know the difference.

### The non-negotiables

- **Electrical safety.** In England, a rental needs an [Electrical Installation Condition Report](https://mjmaintenance.co.uk/electrician-gloucester) at least every five years, and at change of tenancy if the existing one has lapsed. I'm City & Guilds 2391 qualified for electrical testing and inspection, so I can carry out the EICR, flag what needs sorting, and fix it without bringing in a separate firm.
- **Fuse board and wiring faults.** That tripping consumer unit a tenant keeps mentioning is worth looking at properly rather than resetting and hoping. Older boards sometimes need upgrading to meet current standards.
- **Water and heating.** A slow leak behind a cupboard does more quiet damage than a dramatic burst. Catching it early is cheaper than replacing a floor.

### The ones that protect the building

- **Roof checks.** Gloucester winters find every weak slate and tired bit of mortar. Booking a [roof inspection](https://mjmaintenance.co.uk/roofing-gloucester) in the warmer months is smart, because sealants, mortar and roofing adhesives cure far better in warm, dry conditions than they do in a damp February.
- **Damp and plaster.** Especially in older, solid-wall properties, which brings me to a point a lot of people get wrong.

## Older properties need the right materials, not just any materials

Plenty of Gloucester's rental stock is older, solid-wall housing: Victorian terraces, village cottages, places built long before cavity walls were a thing. These buildings breathe differently from a modern new build.

Here's the honest answer most landlords don't hear until it's too late. Slap modern cement render or standard gypsum over a solid wall that was built to use lime, and you can trap moisture inside the wall. The damp doesn't go away. It just finds somewhere else to show up, usually in the most expensive spot possible.

Older properties need careful, experienced handling and materials designed for their period, lime included. Get that right and the building stays healthy for years. Get it wrong and you're paying twice.

## Turnarounds between tenancies

The gap between one tenant leaving and the next moving in is where a good maintenance team earns its keep. Every empty week is money you're not getting back, so the work needs to be organised, not improvised.

A typical void-period to-do list might include:

1. **Safety checks first.** EICR if it's due, plus any electrical faults sorted while the place is empty.
2. **The boring stuff before the pretty stuff.** Leaks, damp, dodgy sealant, loose sockets. Fix what's hidden before you touch what's visible.
3. **Refresh the tired rooms.** A simple bathroom refresh is around one week of actual work, which is handy to know when you're planning a changeover.
4. **Plastering and decoration.** So the next tenant walks into something that looks cared for.
5. **Handyman snagging.** Shelving, blinds, a sticking door, the ten small things that make a place feel finished.

Doing all of that through one team means the work runs in the right order, with no waiting around for the next contractor to become free.
