### First, work out what your wall actually is

This is the boring stuff before the pretty stuff, and it's the step most people skip. Your wall is either solid or hollow, and that single fact changes everything about how you fix into it.

**Knock on it.** A quick knuckle test tells you a lot.

- A dull, solid thud usually means **masonry**: brick, block, or stone. Common in older Gloucester terraces and solid-wall period homes.
- A hollow, drum-like sound usually means a **stud wall**: plasterboard with timber uprights behind it. Common in newer builds, loft conversions, and internal partitions.

Why does it matter? Think of it like hanging a hammock. On a solid masonry wall you've got something to anchor into pretty much anywhere. On a stud wall, the plasterboard is just the skin. The real strength is in the timber studs hiding behind it, usually spaced around 400mm or 600mm apart, and if you miss them you're relying on the board alone.

### Match the fixing to the wall, not to the shelf

The little screws and plugs that come taped to the back of a flat-pack shelf are rarely the right ones for your particular wall. Here's the honest answer on what actually works.

**On solid masonry walls:**

1. Drill with a masonry bit, on hammer setting if your drill has one.
2. Use the right size wall plug for the screw, pushed in flush.
3. For anything carrying real weight, step up to frame fixings or resin anchors rather than cheap plastic plugs.

**On plasterboard stud walls:**

1. Find the studs first. A cheap stud detector costs less than a takeaway and saves a wall full of trial holes.
2. Screw straight into the timber stud wherever you can. That's your strongest fixing by a mile.
3. Where you can't hit a stud, use proper plasterboard fixings: spring toggles or metal self-drive anchors for heavier loads, not the flimsy push-in plastic ones for anything you care about.

A quick word on older Gloucester homes. Solid-wall period properties can have soft old brick, crumbly lime plaster, or patches where someone before you already made a mess. They need careful handling and the right materials rather than brute force and a fat screw. Drill too aggressively into tired old masonry and you can blow a chunk out of the face. Slow and steady wins here.

### The things people get wrong (and how to dodge them)

Ask yourself these before you pick up the drill:

- **Is it level?** Measure, mark, and use a spirit level or a level app. A shelf that's a few millimetres out will have everything rolling to one end, and your eye will catch it forever after.
- **What's behind the wall?** This is the big one. Electrical cables usually run vertically up and down from sockets and switches, and water pipes aren't far behind. Drilling blind above a socket is how a quick job turns into an emergency. A cheap detector that finds live cables and pipes is worth its weight in gold.
- **How much weight is really going on it?** A row of paperbacks is one thing. A stack of hardbacks, a marble clock, or your record collection is another. Books are heavier than people think.
- **Are your fixings spread properly?** Two brackets close together will sag in the middle. Support the ends and, on a long run, add a bracket in the middle.

The cost of getting this wrong isn't just a wonky shelf. Like I say on our bathroom jobs, rushing or skipping a step usually shows up later as a crack, a leak, or something that pulls clean out of the wall and takes your plaster with it.

### When a shelf is really a bigger job

Sometimes what you think is a ten-minute shelf turns into something worth doing properly.

- **Floating shelves** look clean and simple, but they hide all their support inside the wall, so the fixing has to do all the work with no visible bracket to help. They're far less forgiving of a weak wall.
- **Alcove shelving** either side of a chimney breast is a lovely feature in a Gloucester terrace, but those alcove walls can be an odd mix of brick, old plaster, and whatever a previous owner patched in.
- **Heavy loads**: a run of shelves for a home gym, a big TV and kit, or kitchen storage carrying full jars and crockery all day.

### Common questions

**Can you hang a heavy shelf on a plasterboard wall?**
Yes, within reason, as long as you fix into the timber studs behind the board or use the right heavy-duty plasterboard anchors. The plasterboard on its own won't hold much, so the trick is finding the solid timber and spreading the load across good fixings.

**How much weight can a normal shelf hold?**
It depends far more on the wall and the fixings than on the shelf itself. A well-fixed bracket into solid masonry will carry a lot. The same bracket into a single plug in crumbly old plaster might not hold a row of mugs. That's why I always want to see the wall before I promise anything.

**My walls are old and the plaster feels soft. Is that a problem?**
It's common in older Gloucester homes, and it just means being careful. Soft lime plaster and tired brick need the right materials and a gentle approach rather than forcing big screws in. Done properly it's no issue at all, but it's not the wall to learn on.

**Can you put a shelf anywhere I want?**
Almost, but not blindly over cables or pipes. Those usually track up and down from sockets and switches, so I check with a detector first and adjust the position a little if needed. A shelf a couple of centimetres to the side beats drilling through a live cable every time.
